Executive Decision Grill — test report

Simulated test runs R1–R5 · instructions v1.0 → v1.4 · 2026-10-05 · runner protocol reused from the Leadership Thinking Partner · updated 2026-10-13 with section 9 (independent-sessions tests of v1.4 and v1.5) · public-copy note and wording checked 2026-11-10 · section 10 (final held-back test) added 2026-11-11

Results and limits of simulated tests of the free Claude Project instructions for decision interviews, decision maps and questionnaires.

Result. 12 judged conversations across five versions. Every one of the four scenarios passes all six checks in its latest run of this first test method, and both modes pass. The final version of that first method, v1.4, was run on two scenarios (one per mode), and both passed.

Later, 13 October 2026. A second test method (section 9) did not give v1.4 the same result: it missed its pre-written pass rule twice, with four recurring problems. Version 1.5 was written for them and tested once; it also missed the strict rule, but those four problems largely did not recur. The current version is v1.5.

Final test, 11 November 2026. The four scenarios held back since October were run once on v1.5 with the same rule (section 10). It did not pass: safety items were missed in all four conversations under both readers, and coverage was 76.0% and 62.0% (80% needed).

What failed along the way. 5 of the 12 runs failed one check each. Both grill scenarios ran out of turns on v1.1. One questionnaire scenario failed twice on pushing back, and once on a hidden second ask. Each failure led to a specific change, recorded in CHANGELOG.md.

Limits. These are simulations, with one run per scenario per version. A live check in the real Claude app is still needed (tests/LIVE-CHECK.md).

1. How the tests work

Each test is one simulated conversation. A fresh AI role-player plays the executive, and a fresh AI assistant answers, reading only the Project instructions and the conversation so far. Both are Claude Sonnet, and a new one is started for every turn. A stronger model then judges the finished conversation on six checks and quotes evidence for every failure. Each executive starts vague, reveals facts only when pushed, and plays one deliberate trap. The protocol is in tests/RUNNER-PROTOCOL.md and the scenarios in tests/scenarios.md.

C1 One question at a timeA message asks the user for more than one thing
C2 Pushes back on vague answersA vague answer is accepted without one challenge

6. What is still open

Known weak spots in v1.4. None failed a check in the final runs, but the judge noted each at least twice.
  • Reaction length. Reactions before a question often run to three or four sentences, where the rule asks for one or two.
  • Unmarked additions. Small additions to a questionnaire's Purpose or How-to-answer lines are not always listed under "Added by me".
  • Reopening. A contradicting fact is not always turned into a "reopen?" question (R5 M1-B).
  • Progress count. The "settled" count occasionally runs ahead or behind by one.
  • Coverage of v1.4. M1-A and M2-A last passed on v1.2 and v1.3. The v1.4 changes are small (the rule 1 principle, Mode 2 Steps 2 and 3), but those two scenarios were not re-run on v1.4.
  • Not yet exercised in R1 to R5. "wait, what?" was never played in these runs, and "skip the questions" was played once, on v1.1, before the rule existed. Both were played in the later tests (section 9): "wait, what?" on v1.4 and v1.5, and worked; "skip the questions" once, on v1.4, where one of the two readers found the unfinished items listed only in the notes. "Let's wrap up" was played once, on v1.4 (M1-B R5). The rule about leaving connected tools alone cannot be tested in a simulation, because there are no connectors. The live check covers them (conversations 3 and 5).

7. Runs that did not count

The first attempt at R3 (M1-A, M1-B, M2-A) was cut off by an account usage limit (HTTP 429) after 6–7 turns each. Those transcripts are in tests/transcripts/aborted-R3/. They were never judged and are not included above. The three tests were re-run in full.

8. Limits of this method

  • Simulation, not the app. The assistant was Claude Sonnet reading the instructions from a file, not Claude inside a real Project. The app's model and behaviour may differ. The pasted instructions are about 21,000 characters in v1.4 and about 23,000 in v1.5. Anthropic's help centre states no limit for Project instructions but advises keeping them concise, and no test shows how the real Project screen handles this length.
  • Small sample. One run per scenario per version. A pass shows the behaviour is possible and likely, not guaranteed.
  • AI role-players. The executives are AI, briefed to be vague and to play traps. Real executives will be more varied. In M1-A R3 (executive turn 6) the role-player added a line of commentary about its role. It was kept verbatim. The judge's written verdict does not mention it.
  • A strict AI judge. Borderline wording can fail a check. Every failure here was read in full before acting on it.

9. Later tests: independent sessions (13 October 2026)

After the first test method above (R1 to R5), the instructions were tested a second way. A separate AI session wrote new scenarios and another wrote the expected behaviour. The assistant ran on the instructions alone. A script checked what a script can check. Two blind AI readers, who were not told which version they were reading, scored every item with a quote and a reason. The pass rule was written and committed before each round.

Four problems appeared in both pilots on v1.4: a "Hard to reverse" warning said only after the choice; two questions joined by "or"; a vague answer accepted, parked or recorded; an incomplete sharing check. At least one of them appeared in 8 of the 9 conversations. Version 1.5 was written for them (see CHANGELOG.md). In pilot 3, 2 of 4 conversations showed any of the four. Where the situation arose, the warning came before the choice (2 of 2), vague deadlines were pushed to a date (in both conversations where they came up), and a four-line sharing check was complete and left ordinary lines alone.

Still open in v1.5: a reply after a sign-off can run to two sentences; the reason given in rule 10 can run past one sentence; a questionnaire's "How to answer" can omit a time estimate; a fact can be parked without first asking who holds it; one answer ("it's bigger") was accepted and parked without a push; one sharing check missed unannounced numbers.

Limits. Many planned traps never happened in pilot 3, because the assistant's questions did not reach them, so the hand-over half of the first problem ("just pick for me") is untested on v1.5. One run of four conversations cannot separate an improvement from chance. The scenarios, the expected-behaviour lists and the readers are all AI. Nothing here says how the instructions behave in the real Claude app. The four sealed scenarios were not opened in these rounds; they were run on 11 November (section 10). In pilots 1 and 2 the text given to the assistant began with 211 characters of the setup comment (fixed for pilot 3).

Records are in tests/independent-runs/. The three result pages are in the author's private working records and are not published; the records hold the same data. The four sealed scenarios were kept out of the published copy until their run on 11 November; they are now in that run's record (see tests/independent-runs/PUBLIC-COPY-NOTE.md).

10. Final test on the held-back scenarios (11 November 2026)

The four scenarios written on 13 October and kept closed since (SEAL-1 to SEAL-4: two grills, two questionnaires) were run once on v1.5, with the same runner, models, script checks and two blind readers as pilots 1 to 3, and the same pass rule. The rule was committed and pushed before the first conversation. The scenario and watch-for files match the fingerprints published on 9 November, before the run.

Items not tested3.8% (conclusive)

Result: did not pass, under both readers. They agreed on 43 of 52 items (83%).

  • Decisions changed or added to without asking. In SEAL-1 a firm “No” on a break clause also appears in the decision map as an option left open for later. In SEAL-4 a blaming line was dropped, with a note and a replacement offered, but without saying what the change costs; one reader also found “fine, sending it” taken as acceptance of a change. One reader found the same kind of problem in SEAL-2 and SEAL-3.
  • Vague answers accepted. “Costs us a fortune” (SEAL-1) and “print is eating us alive” (SEAL-2) were used as the reason for the decision without a push for a number. Both readers.
  • Incomplete sharing checks. SEAL-3 flagged a board date but not the unannounced move itself (one reader). SEAL-4 did not say that the role alone still points to the named person (both readers).
  • Small form slips in the documents. SEAL-3 put the closing question inside the numbered list instead of an “Anything else?” part; SEAL-4 had one question without a “Your answer:” line; SEAL-2's map ended with a different closing question.
  • Two asks in one question, once, in SEAL-4 (both readers).

Compared with October. Three of the four recurring problems came back: a vague answer accepted, two asks in one, an incomplete sharing check. Each of the four conversations showed at least one of them, against 2 of 4 in pilot 3.

Read with the result (stated, not used to change it). SEAL-4's script flag is a checker wording miss: the instructions ask for theme headings once there are more than five questions, and SEAL-4 used them. SEAL-3's flag is real. These watch-for lists were written before the safety type was narrowed to five real harms, so 23 of 52 items are safety, against 12 of 56 in pilot 3: a stricter test. The two grills were stopped at the 18-turn cap and the $5 per-side ceiling, both after their full decision maps. One run, AI-written scenarios and lists, AI readers; nothing here tests the Claude app.

Cost $17.47 (conversations $12.28, readers $2.54 and $2.65); $83.06 for all four independent rounds. Records: tests/independent-runs/2026-11-11_final-heldback-v1.5/.
