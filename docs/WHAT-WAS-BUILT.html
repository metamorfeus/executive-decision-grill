Executive Decision Grill — what was built

Product record · version 1.5 · written 2026-10-05, updated 2026-11-10 · Metamorfeus · adapted from methods in Matt Pocock's skills (MIT licence)

Free Claude Project instructions for CEOs, CFOs, COOs, directors and managers, built to question a business decision, produce a decision map and draft a questionnaire for whoever holds the missing answers—with nothing to install.

Who it is for. Non-technical executives: CEOs, CFOs, COOs, directors and managers, such as a head of sales or an operations director. It was written with the author's clients, course buyers and book readers in mind. A companion to the Leadership Thinking Partner. Nothing to install.

State. Version 1.5, tested only in simulated conversations. In the first test method (12 judged conversations, versions 1.0 to 1.4), each of the four scenarios passed all six checks in its latest run. In the later independent-sessions tests, version 1.4 and then version 1.5 missed the strict pass rule. The four problems version 1.5 was written for appeared far less often (2 of 4 conversations, against 8 of 9 for version 1.4). On 11 November 2026 the final test on the four held-back scenarios did not pass either: safety items were missed in all four conversations, and a decision was changed or added to without asking in at least three of them (section 10 of tests/TEST-REPORT.html). The live check in the Claude app has not been run; the owner chose to publish before it.

1. What it does

Once the instructions are pasted into a Claude Project, Claude:

  • lists the smaller decisions hiding inside the executive's decision, and works through them in the order that settles the most other questions;
  • asks one question at a time, each with "My recommendation:", so "agreed" is a full answer;
  • shows progress as a counter ("[Q4 · 3 of 7 settled]") and writes the decision map by question 15 at the latest;
  • sharpens words that could mean two things, and offers three options that differ in kind when the executive is stuck;
  • flags decisions that are hard to reverse and is built not to accept "whatever you think" on them;
  • is meant to ask before reopening a settled decision when a later fact contradicts it (it did in one test run and missed it in the last);
  • uses only what the executive gives it, and leaves connected email, files and calendar alone unless asked;
  • in the questionnaire, asks about the send, not the subject: who it is for, what they know, what the executive needs back;
  • is built to decline to turn a questionnaire into a judgement of named colleagues: it explains why, offers an alternative and asks;
  • runs a sharing check before anything leaves the executive's office.

3. The fourteen standing rules

#RuleIn short
1One question per message, with your recommendationEach message has one question mark. Any sentence that asks the user to answer, confirm, check or choose counts as a question. "My recommendation:" after every decision question. "Whatever works" accepts it, except on hard-to-reverse decisions.
2Keep it short and keep it movingClaude reacts in one or two sentences, and each mode reaches its output within fifteen questions. "Let's wrap up" goes straight to the output.
3Push back on vague answersClaude pushes back up to twice on a decision and once on a fact, then parks the item or records it as open.
4Judge in neutral, factual wordsClaude says what your answer proves and what it doesn't. It does not praise you, and a short list of banned phrases guards against it.
5I decide, and my words go in as I said themClaude quotes you exactly, marks anything it adds, and asks before it reopens or changes anything.
6Use only what I give youClaude is built not to search your connected tools unless you ask, and not to invent facts.
7Plain business languageClaude avoids code, engineering and consulting jargon, and uses numbers instead of adjectives.
8"Wait, what?"Claude re-explains in three short sentences, then asks the question again.
9Respect people's privacyClaude is built not to help track people's activity. A questionnaire asks for knowledge, not judgements of named people, and goes to one person, not to all staff.
10Requests the instructions rule outClaude gives a one-sentence reason, offers an alternative and asks you. Your request stays visible.
11If I get impatientIf you say "wrap up", Claude goes straight to the output. If you say "skip ahead", it first asks the two most important questions.
12Keep the shareable part cleanClaude puts its own notes after the document, so the document stays clean.
13Before anything leaves my officeClaude reads the whole document once and lists every line you may not want to send.
14Check before you sendClaude checks each question and each output against a short list before it sends it.

6. What was deliberately left out

  • Rounds of questions. The source asks every answerable question at once. We ask one at a time and kept the recommended answer.
  • The AI looking facts up by itself. In the Claude app that could mean searching an executive's connected mailbox. Facts come only from what the executive gives.
  • Beta skills that run AI agents on their own (chief-of-staff, claude-handoff), and every coding, testing and set-up skill.
  • Teach, loop-me and handoff as modes. Their best ideas are recorded in the value review for later.
  • Verbatim source text. Everything was rewritten. NOTICE.md carries the MIT notice anyway.

7. How well it works

ModeScenario AScenario B
Decision GrillPASS CFO, month-end close (v1.0 and v1.2)PASS Head of sales, dropping a reseller, "just pick for me" (v1.2 and v1.4)
QuestionnairePASS Ops director to head of IT, "his team rushed it" (v1.3)PASS Head of sales to his regional manager, "rate them on loyalty" (v1.1 and v1.4)

Six checks were scored on every conversation. Across all 12 runs: no flattery, never changing direction without asking, and plain language passed 12 of 12; one question at a time passed 11 of 12; pushing back and ending with the output passed 10 of 12. Every failure led to a change recorded in CHANGELOG.md.

Known weak spots. Reactions before a question still sometimes run to three or four sentences instead of two. Small additions to a questionnaire's Purpose line are not always listed under "Added by me". In the last grill run, a fact that contradicted a settled decision was noted but not turned into a "reopen?" question. In the first test method the CFO scenario and the operations-director scenario were last run on v1.2 and v1.3. "Wait, what?" was played in the later tests below and worked. "Skip the questions" was played once, on v1.4, where one of the two readers found the unfinished items listed only in the notes. The rule about leaving connected tools alone cannot be simulated, so the live check covers it. Full detail is in tests/TEST-REPORT.html.
Later tests, 13 October 2026 (version 1.4, then version 1.5). Separate AI sessions wrote new test scenarios, played the executive, and scored the answers without knowing which version they were reading. Three rounds were run. Version 1.4 did not meet the strict pass rule that was written before each round, in two rounds. Four problems showed up in both: a "Hard to reverse" warning given only after you had chosen, two questions joined by "or", a vague answer accepted, and an incomplete sharing check. Version 1.5 was written for those four. On four new scenarios it again missed the strict pass rule (one missing detail in a questionnaire, one scenario below the floor, and about a quarter of the checks could not be tested because some planned moments never came up). But the four problems appeared in 2 of 4 conversations, against 8 of 9 for version 1.4. Version 1.5 is the current version. It has not been tested in the real Claude app. Details, with the cost ($65.59 for all three rounds) and the limits, are in section 9 of tests/TEST-REPORT.html and in three result pages in the author's private working records (not published; the records in tests/independent-runs/ hold the same data).
